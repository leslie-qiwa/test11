Last year I set out to build an interactive learning activity from scratch — no template, no pre-existing framework, just a problem I wanted to solve and a blank page. What followed was one of the most instructive experiences I've had as both a designer and an educator.

## Starting with the Problem, Not the Solution

The temptation when building something new is to start with the format. I wanted to resist that. Instead, I spent the first two weeks just observing — watching where students lost focus, where instructions caused confusion, and where engagement was highest.

What I found: students were most engaged when they had a concrete, low-stakes decision to make. Not reading about something, not watching something — deciding something.

## The First Prototype Was Wrong

My first version was too complicated. I had six steps, three branching paths, and a reflection component at the end. In testing, students spent more time understanding the structure than doing the actual learning.

The fix was radical simplification. I cut it to three steps, removed the branching, and made the reflection optional. Engagement immediately improved.

## What the Process Taught Me

Good instructional design is really just good subtraction. You start with everything you think students need and then remove everything that isn't absolutely necessary. What remains is usually a much better activity than what you started with.

The other lesson: test early and test with real students, not colleagues. Teachers will complete an activity politely even when it's confusing. Students will tell you exactly where it breaks — not in words, but in behaviour.

## The Result

The finished activity is now part of my portfolio. It's been used in four different classroom settings with consistently positive feedback. Not because it's clever — but because it's simple enough to get out of the way of the learning.
