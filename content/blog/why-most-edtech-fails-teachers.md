There's a running joke among educators: every September brings a new tool that's going to change everything, and every June those same tools are forgotten. Having watched this cycle repeat for years, I've started to notice the patterns that separate ed-tech that sticks from ed-tech that doesn't.

## The Problem Isn't the Technology

The tools themselves are rarely the issue. The problem is almost always a mismatch between what a tool was designed to do and the real conditions of a classroom. Ed-tech built by people who haven't spent time in classrooms tends to optimise for demos, not for daily use.

## What Failing Tools Have in Common

- **They require more time to set up than they save.** If a teacher spends forty minutes configuring a tool to deliver a ten-minute activity, the maths doesn't work.
- **They assume reliable infrastructure.** Slow Wi-Fi, shared devices, and inconsistent browser versions will break most tools at the worst possible moment.
- **They're built for one use case.** Tools that can only do one thing well rarely justify the cognitive load of introducing something new to students.

## What the Good Tools Get Right

The tools that genuinely last in classrooms tend to share a few qualities: they're forgiving of mistakes, they work on the first try without training, and they have a clear answer to the question "what problem does this solve for a teacher at 7pm on a Sunday?"

The best ed-tech is humble. It doesn't try to replace the teacher — it removes friction so the teacher can do more of what only they can do.

## The Takeaway

Before adopting any new tool, I now ask one question: can a student use this successfully within three minutes of seeing it for the first time? If the answer is no, the barrier to entry is probably too high for regular classroom use.

The tools that pass that test are the ones worth your time.
