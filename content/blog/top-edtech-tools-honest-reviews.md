Every year the ed-tech landscape expands. New platforms launch with bold promises, veteran tools ship major updates, and somewhere in the middle of it all, teachers are expected to figure out what's actually worth their time. This post is my honest attempt to help with that.

I've spent the past twelve months genuinely using — not just demoing — the tools below in educational contexts. Here's what I found.

---

## 1. Canva for Education — ★★★★☆

**What it promises:** A visual design platform that makes creating classroom materials, student presentations, and educational graphics accessible to everyone.

**What it delivers:** Largely, exactly that. Canva for Education is free for teachers and students, and the template library has grown to a point where it's genuinely useful for quick turnaround work. Presentation decks, infographics, worksheets, and even basic video content are all within reach without a design background.

**Where it falls short:** The AI features feel bolted on rather than integrated, and the free tier has become more restricted over time. Some of the best templates now sit behind a Pro paywall, which creates a confusing experience for students who access the tool independently.

**Verdict:** Still one of the best free tools available to educators. Use it, but set expectations with students about what's available for free.

---

## 2. Notion — ★★★☆☆

**What it promises:** An all-in-one workspace for notes, project management, databases, and collaboration.

**What it delivers:** Notion is extraordinarily powerful — and that's precisely its problem in an educational context. The learning curve for students who haven't used it before is steep. I spent more time teaching Notion than using it to teach anything else during the first month of introducing it to a class.

**Where it shines:** For self-directed learners and educators building their own systems, Notion is excellent. My personal lesson planning and resource organisation has genuinely improved since switching to it.

**Verdict:** A great personal tool for educators. A challenging classroom tool for most students unless you have time to invest in onboarding.

---

## 3. Quizlet — ★★★★☆

**What it promises:** Digital flashcards and study games that make memorisation more engaging.

**What it delivers:** Quizlet remains one of the most reliable tools in the ed-tech space. The core flashcard and study mode functionality works exactly as expected, students are already familiar with it, and the shared sets feature means teachers can publish ready-to-use revision resources instantly.

**Where it falls short:** The AI-generated quiz questions are inconsistent in quality. I've seen Quizlet suggest factually incorrect answers in auto-generated tests, which is a real problem if students trust the content without checking.

**Verdict:** Use it for student-created flashcard sets and teacher-published revision resources. Avoid leaning on the AI generation features until the accuracy improves.

---

## 4. Padlet — ★★★★★

**What it promises:** A digital board for collaborative posting — text, images, links, video, and more — in a visual, flexible format.

**What it delivers:** Everything it promises, and then some. Padlet has become my most-used classroom tool this year. The interface is intuitive enough that students are contributing within minutes of first use, the moderation tools give teachers appropriate oversight, and the range of layouts (wall, stream, map, shelf, timeline) means it works for an unusually wide range of activities.

**Where it falls short:** The free tier limits you to three boards, which will frustrate regular users quickly. The paid plan is reasonably priced for schools but adds friction for individual educators.

**Verdict:** Exceptional tool. If your school can fund it, prioritise this one.

---

## 5. Google Classroom — ★★★☆☆

**What it promises:** A simple, integrated platform for managing assignments, feedback, and communication between teachers and students.

**What it delivers:** Google Classroom does the basics reliably, and its integration with the rest of Google Workspace makes it the path of least resistance for schools already in that ecosystem. Assignment distribution, submission collection, and grade return all work without fuss.

**Where it falls short:** The platform has barely evolved in years. The rubric tool is limited, the gradebook is basic, and the feedback options feel underpowered compared to what modern educators need. It works, but it doesn't inspire.

**Verdict:** Dependable but uninspiring. A solid backbone for school administration, not a tool that will transform learning on its own.

---

## Final Thoughts

No single tool will fix a classroom, and the best ed-tech is always the kind that disappears into the background and lets teaching happen. The tools that earned the highest marks this year were the ones that students could pick up without a tutorial — Padlet and Canva especially.

The tools I'd be most cautious about recommending wholesale are those that demand significant setup time from teachers who are already stretched thin.
